## Part A — Architecture and purchase decision

**Keep the inline shoulder roll; pursue D20 belt drives at pitch and elbow. Do not buy an all-inline arm for this robot at this budget.** Inline helps where removing an off-axis motor solves a particular packaging problem. The shoulder roll is a good example: the CAD dome goes from **148 × 211 to 121 × 96 mm**, and across-body grasp coverage goes from **52% to 60%** (`roll_inline.md`). There is no comparable demonstrated benefit at shoulder pitch. D20 is the least expensive *candidate* that meets the heavy-carry requirements, not a qualified design: it still needs a genuine 24 V operating point, a reliable belt stage, and a loaded test.

**Purchase call: conditional GO for the reduced Step 1 instrumented lever rig; NO-GO for Step 2 or full-arm printing.** One GIM6010-8/SDC104, the DM-J4310, CAN interfaces, test/fixture equipment, catch and remote stop are useful. Get the exact driver SKU confirmed. Release safety-board parts only after the schematic, protection coordination, layout and clamp simulation have been independently reviewed. Do not energize a motor before the motorless board tests. Do not order a batch of GIMs, custom caps, or any of the three proposed inline actuator types to find out whether the first GIM can be controlled safely. Success on an SDC104 is not qualification of an SDC303 or of the D20 driver.

### Which parts of the study I would trust

- **Load and speed:** B0 is a useful test article, not the 20 lb robot. At the specified **0.25 m** hug limit its minimum rated margin is **0.69×**, versus **1.54×** for D20 (`SCORECARD.md`, R2c). The study promotes **0.20 m** to the summary and then declares all inline variants pass. That changes the answer. For I2b, using its 20 N·m rated elbow and the study's 1.72× at 0.20 m, the implied demand is 11.63 N·m. Moving the horizontal 4.54 kg load another 50 mm adds **2.23 N·m**, giving about **1.44×**, *below* 1.5. Rerun the actual poses; don't buy the $470 elbow on the published pass. D20's 1.54× is itself only just over the rule, before unequal load sharing or thermal derating. D20 also trades rated pitch/elbow speed down to **20 rpm**; verify that the **loaded** floor-to-bed trajectory works at minimum bus voltage, not just that a static pose works.
- **Mass and tip:** The detachable arm mass is the wrong number to minimize alone. I2b's piece is **4.64 vs 4.83 kg** for B1, yet its elbow actuator is **0.90 vs 0.40 kg**, its torso-side pitch assembly is **1.80 vs 1.35 kg**, and its straight-arm gravity moment is **10.3 vs 9.5 N·m**. Mass at the elbow axis adds little gravity moment *about the elbow* but matters to the shoulder. Assign housing and rotor to the correct bodies with actual COMs and inertias; do not model an actuator centered on an axis as a hand payload. I1 and I2a already miss A10 at **28.3 and 29.6 mm**. I2b's **30.7 mm** is not a defensible pass with estimated parts and caster behavior. Even B1's **34.7 mm** is a prototype result, not robust stability. Include finished covers, cable loops, ballast tolerances and all heavy-carry modes.
- **Back-drive:** The useful comparison is **whole-joint ratio and rotor inertia**, not “motor” versus “belt.” B1 has 24:1 total at pitch/elbow; the 8108-36 has 36:1. For the assumed same rotor, that is **2.25× the reflected rotor inertia**, not 2.25× the static hand force. \(J_rN^2\) is a good starting point. The back-driving efficiency approximation is not a breakaway-friction law. Grease, seals, gear load, belt tension, temperature, brake/phase state and drive zero-current behavior all matter. I know of no verified measurements for these exact assemblies that justify the quoted **0.6–2.2 N** or even the proposed uncertainty band. A fixed 0.60 or 0.43 m lever is not the hand lever in every pose. Measure both residual gravity and friction; a comfortable gravity-compensated jog is not evidence of low unpowered drag.
- **Collision:** I would **not** mandate ≤10:1, nor approve 36:1 from a ratio alone. Set a limit on directional effective mass **at a specified speed, contact stiffness, body site and pose**. In the study's own fixed-target spring model, \(m_{\max}=(F_{\max}/v)^2/k\). Pip's R17 target is **140 N**, not the study's suggested 280 N. At 0.5 m/s and 75 N/mm the illustrative bound is **1.05 kg**; *every* reach-pose variant predicts **212–236 N**. For B1's 2.61 kg the corresponding speed is **0.316 m/s before any uncertainty allowance**. The hand-contact peak at **9–10 ms** precedes a 14–18 ms detector trip, let alone torque decay. Lower speed and passive compliance can do more for the first peak than changing reducers. Sweep \(1/[n^\mathsf{T}JM^{-1}J^\mathsf{T}n]\) over contact normals, poses, link contact points and payloads. The straight-arm table reports finite “worst” alongside **infinite axial mass**: that is a constrained direction of the rigid model, not a benign finite worst case. Include base motion, structural/skin compliance, active impedance, pressure and sustained trapping; use an instrumented surrogate, not a person. Neither a chest-spring calculation nor a current trip is a safety certification.
- **Backlash and accuracy:** 15 arcmin at a 0.60 m lever is **2.62 mm**. A belt reduction divides upstream gearbox backlash but adds its own stretch and reversal error; removing the belt does not remove gearbox backlash. The **0.9 mm** row is a calibrated joint-sensing RSS estimate, not accuracy at a loaded fingertip. An output encoder can observe lost motion; it cannot make a reversal transmit torque before the teeth take up, or measure deflection downstream of itself. I would use a 2EC output for absolute reference and, **if its bandwidth, latency and data path are demonstrated**, a conservatively tuned outer position loop around a fast inner loop. Do not assume MIT mode closes on that encoder simply because it is present. Test reversals through zero gravity torque for hunting and repeatability.
- **Packaging and simplicity:** The credible benefits are a shorter elbow housing and a much narrower yaw block. The inline elbow is still **~105–107 × 108 mm** across the knee; it is not narrower in every direction. Coaxial yaw trades **175 × 92 mm** plan for about **Ø92 mm** but adds **15–25 mm** of axial drop. The reported **567 mm** stow is a hull substitution in one old pose, with just **3 mm** to the design limit, not a swept, wired, guarded assembly. Keep the pitch balancer and its service access; don't put a Ø96 motor in its volume without first showing where the cord, crank, bearings and spring go. The reduction from **50 pieces/108 fasteners to 14/63** is worthwhile, but the estimated hands-on saving is only **0.6 h per arm** for I2b. Recount against D26: the simplicity text still counts eccentric bushings that `belts.md` says are gone, and the claimed **658 g print saving** needs a *printed-only* mass ledger for the replacement parts. Inline also introduces driver integration and bearing/alignment work.

**The next variant I would study is B1 plus *only* the DM-J4340 yaw.** At the study's low prices the inline yaw is $155 + $25 versus about $155 **including** the B0/B1 belt drive hardware: about **+$57 for the robot** with the allowance, not +$543. That targets a real envelope and tensioning problem, away from the pitch balancer, without adding a 1 kg elbow. It is not yet a selected part: check delivered price, output support, dual-encoder operation, the extra drop, and the yaw thermal result (**6.9 N·m demanded vs 5.9 continuous** in the sleeve model). I would rank a J8009 at the elbow above a 36:1 elbow for manual interaction *if* the real heavy-hold requirement and price could be met. I would not put the 48:1 GIM at yaw to obtain actuator-brand uniformity. If the D20 24 V test fails, reopen the payload, gearing or budget decision; do not silently revert to B0 and retain a 20 lb rating.

### Measurements that would change the decision

1. **Qualify one drive before one arm.** With the output mechanically restrained as appropriate, verify torque mode, signed torque feedback, calibrated torque constant, flash current limit after power cycle, command loss, UVLO, overvoltage and reset behavior. Repeat per driver family. Measure joint torque–speed–current at full and depleted 6S-equivalent voltage, including harness drop. Run representative holds and the two-minute basket cycle in a representative *closed* mount from hot as well as cold.
2. **Compare actual joints, not bare motors.** On a horizontal-axis or gravity-balanced fixture use a calibrated lever/load cell and independent output angle. Log breakaway, slow running torque both ways, torque–angle hysteresis at several loads, and hot/cold results in phases-open, drive-disabled and zero-torque states. Fit output inertia from torque/acceleration with two known added inertias, subtracting fixture inertia and friction. Check encoder zero over power cycles and log motor angle, output angle and actual link motion through reversal. Start with B1 and the roll sample; borrow or obtain a returnable yaw sample before buying mixed drives.
3. **Validate the endpoints.** Weigh components and locate COMs; mock up the guarded elbow fold, pitch balancer and yaw harness. Measure independent hand deflection/error, stop distance, peak force/pressure and post-trip force on an instrumented compliant target at progressively increasing energy. Verify heavy holds at **0.25 m**, basket pick/carry/set-down with unequal shares, floor reactions and tip reserve with a tether. Time assembly, belt adjustment and a motor swap. Release the architecture only on those results **and** a delivered BOM within the owner's limit.
